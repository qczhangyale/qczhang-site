*A machine can exchange its information exactly without being ready to work again. Two quantum descriptions can agree until another system is attached. Fine motion can disappear from a simplified picture while continuing to shape its future. Fourteen research papers explore what must survive the journey from a physical whole to the parts we describe and measure.*

Imagine replacing a complicated physical system with a simpler one.

You average over its fine structure. You retain the large-scale variables. You calculate an energy that correctly describes the states you have chosen to keep. Everything looks ready for a smaller, more manageable theory.

Then you let the system move.

The simplified prediction and the complete motion can part company. Something hidden in the discarded details still influences what happens next. A snapshot that was sufficient for one question was not sufficient for the next.

One of the striking examples in **Complementarity-First Foundational Release IV** goes further: a fine-scale component can become arbitrarily small in amplitude while retaining a finite influence on the coarse dynamics. Its size is shrinking, but the strength of the dynamics acting on it is growing. Ignoring it because it looks small misses what it does.[^memory]

The mathematics has forgotten something the system still uses.

That is one entrance into a release spanning autonomous information exchange, magnetic monopoles, quantum composition, geometric transport, superconducting response, and the emergence of effective motion. The fourteen papers, brought together by the review *Records, Composition, and Controlled Dynamics*, ask a common question without pretending to share one physical model:

> **How much of a physical whole can we leave out before the description stops supporting what we want to do with it?**[^overview]

## From complementary relations to working physics

The earlier *From Relation to Reality* articles traced the programme’s inspiration through the ancient question of unity and multiplicity, the mutually defining forms of the Taichi Diagram, and the paired architecture of DNA.

These images suggested an unfamiliar starting point: perhaps relations are not merely connections between already complete objects. Perhaps the objects themselves acquire their roles within a more fundamental relational whole.

DNA also supplied a warning. Complementary strands do not copy themselves simply because their sequences fit. Replication requires machinery, materials, and energy. A relation can make an operation possible without supplying everything needed to perform it.

That distinction carries into Release IV. The foundational proposal remains an explanatory starting point, not a substitute for a physical theory. The mathematical work must still specify what the systems are, how they interact, what can be prepared, and what a measurement returns.[^foundation]

The new release follows those questions into constructions where the details become consequential. What must a machine retain between operations? Which orientation labels survive quantum composition? When does an omitted mode return as memory? What resources turn a calculated signal into a measurable one?

The programme is moving from asking what structures are possible toward asking **when those structures can actually be used together**.[^overview]

## A machine that works once has not yet learned to repeat

Picture a courier arriving at a processing station. It carries fresh information. The station contains an old result. The task is to replace the station’s contents with the incoming payload and send the courier away with the old output.

In the first source-refresh paper, this story becomes a finite classical Hamiltonian model. Both the information-bearing variables and the moving apparatus belong to the equations. Nothing outside the model is assumed to press a perfectly timed switch.

The construction’s central trick is spatial. As the courier traverses a shaped interaction region, the total exchange is governed by the accumulated spatial pulse area. Under the stated mechanical coupling, passage through the complete region produces the exact register swap. Reaching that region and completing the passage are themselves proved for a bounded family of prepared inputs.[^refresh]

The result is more than a suggestive circuit diagram. It is a specified autonomous motion with a common readout and a quantitative comparison for the two output ports.

But follow the courier after the exchange.

It now carries the old result, and its mechanical state is not the state with which it arrived. The information transfer has succeeded; the apparatus has not automatically regained the readiness required for another use.

Think of a delivery mechanism whose package arrives correctly but whose actuator remains extended. A correct delivery and a reset mechanism are different achievements. In the paper, the distinction is not an engineering afterthought: the mechanical endpoint remains part of the dynamical description.[^refresh]

The two companion studies pursue what is needed to connect such operations responsibly. One carries the actual state, time, and interaction history into a carefully bounded reference calculation. The other separates the intended equations from the exact piecewise equations represented by stored constants and from their floating-point execution.

An apparently tiny numerical detail can matter here. A stored number intended to mark a full rotation need not be exactly a full rotation. In the examined model, that difference produces small but nonzero changes in the energy accounting at interfaces. The result is not evidence of a new dissipation mechanism. It identifies a difference between the model intended and the mathematical source actually implemented.[^refresh]

The trilogy does not finish an indefinitely reusable machine. It supplies a one-use construction and several of the precise conditions needed to connect preparation, motion, and subsequent use.

Its lesson is concrete: **an operation acts on its apparatus as well as on its data.**

## The magnetic knot that does not disappear when the totals cancel

The magnetic-charge papers move to a very different kind of whole.

A magnetic monopole is the theoretical counterpart of an isolated magnetic pole. The release does not report discovering one. Instead, it asks how a proposed magnetic exterior can fit into a smooth interior while preserving a specified complementary structure.

At first, the mathematics seems to offer an easy escape. Two oppositely charged geometric sectors have topological contributions that cancel in the combined complex description.

Yet the cancellation does not settle the problem.

The required rule pairing the sectors retains a real geometric structure, and that structure can still carry an obstruction to smooth extension. Removing the pairing would simplify the bookkeeping—but would also change what is being extended. It is a little like declaring two twists cancelled after erasing the markings that specify how the twisted pieces must remain connected.[^magnetic]

The first paper identifies the enlargement needed in its stated construction and develops a smooth core within a separately supplied field theory. It then asks what the exterior actually reproduces. Matching magnetic flux is not enough: energy density, field stresses, and transport along paths have their own comparisons.

That distinction matters because a model can get the total charge right while getting other physical effects wrong.

The second paper studies disturbances around the supplied core. Its reported results control the perturbation energy across angular sectors after allowing for collective motions such as translating the core. But a neutral radiative sector remains gapless: the kinetic spectrum still reaches arbitrarily low energies. An energy-control statement does not magically impose a positive minimum energy on every disturbance.[^magnetic]

The third paper moves to nonlinear constraints and regular local evolution. It constructs momenta satisfying the constraints and a prescribed classical electric charge at the actual perturbed configuration—not merely at an idealized background. For sufficiently regular data in the stated class, the resulting dynamics preserves the relevant constraints over a positive time interval.

This is a substantial sequence: construct a core, control specified disturbances, and establish constrained local motion. It is not a prediction of how many monopoles nature contains, an explanation of non-detection, or a proof that every allowed disturbance stays controlled forever.[^magnetic]

The important advance is that “the monopole exists in the model” has been separated into several questions that can be answered individually.

## When changing the description changes nothing—and when it does

Suppose a map is reflected left to right. If its coordinates, labels, and every instruction are transformed consistently, it can still describe the same journey. Reflect only the map while keeping the original instructions, and the journey can go wrong.

The quantum-composition papers investigate a more subtle mathematical version of this distinction.

One paper studies a rule that changes how an ordered quantum state space is represented using selected matrix transpositions. When states, measurements, and processes are all translated together, the completed construction is equivalent to the supplied ordinary quantum theory. A new presentation is not automatically a new physical theory.

But the labels become important when systems are combined. The raw rule treats one side of a composition differently from the other. With three components, different bracketings can produce different admitted state sets. Retaining the orientation information allows the construction to be repaired coherently; identifying certain labels too soon can make the raw composition rule ill-defined.[^composition]

Information that seemed redundant for a system in isolation was needed when another system was attached.

The paper also distinguishes translating a description from applying a physical operation to an unknown state. Full matrix transpose is not a universally available quantum channel on a fixed nontrivial system. Changing the mathematical presentation does not give a laboratory a machine that performs every map used in that translation.[^composition]

A second study explores reciprocal geometric cells. A simple analogy helps: a circle and its surrounding square cast the same horizontal and vertical shadows, but the square contains corner points the circle does not. Knowing each separate projection does not identify the complete shape.

The paper develops a related distinction between a multidimensional ball and a product of disks. Their individual planar projections agree, while their global volumes differ. It also shows why assigning a geometric cell a symplectic size does not select a smallest physical quantum of action. The geometry organizes possibilities; it does not, by itself, choose Planck’s constant or a quantum state space.[^cells]

The third study follows frames around finite networks. Rotations alone add their angles in the familiar way. Insert a reflection, however, and the sense of a later rotation can reverse. A reflected coordinate system does not treat clockwise and counterclockwise as the original one did.

This changes how transport must be composed. The paper constructs exact consistency relations using shared edges, connecting paths, and ordered products. It checks specified refinements, including every triangulation of a pentagon and a hexagon, while preserving the declared boundary transport.

A particularly vivid example is an annulus—a region with a hole. All the small face loops can be flat, yet transport around the hole retains a reflection. Local tests have not exhausted the global information.[^holonomy]

These results do not establish invariance of every possible geometry or physical action. They show exactly which information a finite transport construction must preserve for its particular comparison to remain meaningful.

## Two superconducting models can answer the first question identically

A gentle probe does not ask every question a material can answer.

As an analogy, two mechanical objects might respond alike to a slight push but differently when driven harder. The superconductivity study turns that general idea into two explicitly defined model comparisons.

In its restricted density-response setting, two different distributions of coupling weights give identical linear responses when the relevant first two moments agree. At cubic order, a fourth-moment contribution appears. A nonlinear question can therefore reveal a distinction that the original linear question missed.[^superconductivity]

But even this does not create a universal microscopic fingerprint. An additional interaction channel changes the cubic response. Smooth alternatives can also approach the comparison model closely enough to remove a uniform separation. Distinguishing one pair is not the same as distinguishing every member of a broad family.

The optical part of the study asks a separately specified question. Its models retain their actual band motion, pairing response, electric currents, and electromagnetic environment, and they are compared under the same incoming field. Equal incoming light does not license independently choosing whatever internal field is convenient in each model.

For a selected seventh-degree response coefficient, the work supplies a numerical enclosure that excludes zero, together with bounds on selected higher-order corrections and the remaining tail. “Seventh degree” refers to the power of the driving amplitudes in the expansion; it is not simply another name for seventh-harmonic light. Lower optical responses can already differ.[^superconductivity]

The result is a controlled mathematical distinction—not a measured signal.

That last step is especially important. A detector generally measures intensity, whereas the calculation may isolate a complex field component. A suitably prepared reference wave can make an intensity difference sensitive to that component, but its phase, waveform, and calibration must be supplied. The certified bounds also concern very small normalized amplitudes; they do not establish practical detectability.[^superconductivity]

A calculation can be more precise than an experiment is sensitive. Precision and visibility are not interchangeable.

## Why adding paths requires more than complex numbers

The four Feynman-composition papers return to the foundations of quantum calculation.

Quantum amplitudes play several roles. Sequential processes combine one way; unresolved alternatives combine another; measurement weights require a further rule. Writing everything with complex numbers can make these roles look like one package.

The first paper separates them.

A reciprocal rotation on a supplied real plane admits the familiar algebra of complex multiplication. But the existence of serial transport does not build a device that recombines alternatives, and it does not determine a probability law. Two individually reversible contributions can cancel at an output; their sum need not be another reversible transport.[^amplitudes]

A positive construction begins with a declared quadratic-response model. Calibrated pairwise comparisons test whether the records associated with different alternatives agree. If the relevant differences vanish along a connected comparison network, the responses reduce to coherent addition. A connected tree is enough for this particular comparison task, and imperfect agreement leads to explicit accumulated-error bounds.

This gives a resource account of coherence rather than obtaining interference by renaming a rotation.

Probability weights require another step. Begin with a specified rule assigning normalized weights locally from each channel’s size. Allow a channel to be divided into arbitrary orthogonal subchannels while leaving a comparison channel unchanged, and require the recorded subchannel weights to add back to the original. Those assumptions force the usual weighting by squared amplitude. Equal splitting into two alone leaves room for other functions; the restricted symmetry is not enough.[^amplitudes]

There is a related warning about records. In the supplied path-and-record model, an unconditional operation acting only on the record leaves the path’s reduced state unchanged. Resetting that record alone cannot restore interference missing from the path state. Selecting a measurement outcome or reversing the original joint interaction is a different operation, with different resources.[^amplitudes]

The second paper carries admitted amplitude dynamics toward configuration-space histories in a controlled one-dimensional setting. A history needs more than a phase attached to a route: the factors for transitions, the time ordering, and the measure must all be retained. Under the stated scaling and preparation assumptions, lattice evolution can approach continuum evolution.

But convergence of prepared states does not guarantee that every raw, infinitely sharp kernel value converges point by point. Finite resolution changes the question that can be controlled. The paper supplies a bridge—not a license to interchange every possible continuum limit.[^histories]

## The detail that returns as memory

Now return to the disappearing fine motion with which the story began.

Suppose a model groups neighboring sites into a single coarse variable. The coarse variable keeps their average. A complementary variable keeps their difference.

The difference can be invisible in the average without being dynamically irrelevant. Two contributions with opposite amplitudes cancel in the average, yet still carry positive quadratic intensity. Whether that difference can be neglected depends on the interaction, not merely on the decision to stop displaying it.

The third composition paper gives an exact condition for one natural coarse description, with the local potential constant inside each block, to evolve autonomously: every fine site inside a block must have the same appropriately normalized coupling rate toward each other block. When that condition fails, motion can leave the chosen coarse subspace.[^memory]

One can still eliminate the unwanted variables mathematically. But elimination does not abolish their influence.

The exact coarse equation acquires a memory term involving the past coarse motion, together with a contribution from the fine variables’ initial state. The complete dynamics remains finite and conservative. No random bath has been introduced, and no irreversible loss of coherence has been proved.

The apparent memory belongs to the reduced description. The variables that carried the missing information have been solved for, not physically destroyed.[^memory]

This is where “small” becomes a dangerous shortcut. In the paper’s refining-chain example, the omitted contrast can shrink while the dynamics acting on it becomes stronger. A small amplitude does not guarantee a small energy effect—just as, by analogy, a small displacement against an increasingly stiff spring need not cost little energy.

Even the way we measure the remaining state can change. Choosing the fine configuration of lowest energy for each coarse value produces both an effective energy and an induced metric: a new rule for measuring the size and overlap of the retained states. Keeping the corrected energy but discarding the corrected metric uses only half of the reduction.[^memory]

The consequences reach the familiar question of whether an approximation is accurate. The work obtains strong low-energy spectral estimates, yet a general prepared-state estimate can be weaker. Getting the frequencies right does not automatically mean that the chosen preparation follows the right motion.

By adding a prescribed fine-scale correction—a mathematical “dressing”—the approximation improves for an isolated low mode under quantitative spectral separation. The next paper develops common preparation maps that work on entire energy windows, with their own assumptions and error bounds.[^effective]

The positive result is not that simplification fails. It is that a useful simplified theory can be built more carefully once the omitted structure is accounted for.

## Knowing the slow motion does not prepare it

A low-energy theory usually begins by assuming the system is already in the sector it describes.

How does it get there?

The fourth composition paper makes that preparation question explicit. A short-range Hamiltonian can have low-energy modes spread across an entire system. A map that accurately completes an already low-energy state is not automatically a local filter that selects such states from arbitrary inputs.[^selection]

An analogy comes from sound. Knowing the bass line of a recording and isolating it from an arbitrary mixture are different tasks. A frequency-selective filter must resolve the distinctions the requested separation depends on.

In the paper’s specified one-dimensional setting, bounded polynomial filters face a quantitative spatial cost. When the required transition between accepted and rejected energies is kept fixed in physical units, the theorem prevents their physical support from shrinking to zero under mesh refinement, subject to its stated path and spectral assumptions.

Making the grid finer does not, by itself, make the same physical selection arbitrarily local.[^selection]

There are constructive alternatives. The paper develops finite-range approximations with explicit errors and distinguishes accepted, transition, and rejected spectral sectors. Certain inverse-operator approximations admit bounded physical support. A separate correction can restore positivity to a certified Hermitian approximation without enlarging its support bound, although it changes the error budget.

The resulting picture connects preparation, normalization, time evolution, and readout. Each can be controlled, but each contributes something that the other stages do not supply for free.[^selection]

## What Release IV adds

The five branches are not five proofs of the same theorem. A courier’s mechanical state, a magnetic bundle’s winding, a quantum orientation label, and an eliminated fine coordinate are different objects. Their shared vocabulary does not make them one substance.

Their connection is a sharper habit of asking physical questions.

A successful exchange prompts a question about the next use. A smooth magnetic core prompts questions about perturbations and constrained evolution. Equivalent quantum descriptions prompt questions about composition and fixed references. Linear agreement prompts a different probe. A good effective equation prompts a question about preparing the states on which it works.[^overview]

Several of the release’s most useful results identify precisely where a tempting shortcut fails. But they also show what to do next: preserve the apparatus endpoint, retain the needed labels, supply the appropriate constraints, keep the memory and metric, or pay the spatial cost of selection.

The remaining frontiers are correspondingly concrete. They include completing the preparation-to-reuse chain, extending local monopole control to long times, connecting model-specific nonlinear contrasts to calibrated experiments, and making low-energy preparation scalable within its stated error and locality requirements. A common physical origin for the branches would require further constructions, not merely a common diagram.[^frontier]

The larger Complementarity-First question remains whether a world of apparently separate things can be understood through relations that are explanatorily prior to those things. Release IV does not settle that question. It develops mathematical ways to test what happens when a whole is divided into the parts we choose to retain.

The goal is not to keep every detail forever. Physics would be impossible without simplification.

It is to discover which details may be left out, which must return in another form, and which become indispensable when the next question is asked.

> **The whole need not be visible in every measurement. But a successful description must retain—or correctly account for—what the next operation still needs.**
